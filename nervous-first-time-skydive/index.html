Nervous First-Time Skydive: A Calm, One-to-One Tandem

Feeling nervous about your first skydive is completely normal. At Tailored Skydive you jump one-to-one with instructor Rich Taylor from Dunkeswell Airfield in Devon, with a calm, unhurried briefing and extra reassurance at every step, so you feel in control. 10,000ft from £275.

Being nervous is completely normal

Almost everyone feels something before their first skydive: excitement, a knot in the stomach, or both. It is a natural reaction to doing something extraordinary, and it is not a sign you should not do it. What matters is that you are not rushed, and that you understand what is about to happen.

That is why Tailored Skydive is one-to-one. You booked a personal experience rather than a high-volume session, so Rich moves at your pace, explains every step, and makes sure you feel comfortable and confident before getting on the plane.

How Rich works with nervous jumpers

  • A calm, unhurried briefing. A one-to-one briefing covers body position, the aircraft exit and what to expect during the flight. No experience is needed.
  • Every sensation explained first. Rich talks you through what you will feel before it happens, so nothing is a surprise.
  • A “Nervous / First-Timer” style. When you design your jump you can choose extra reassurance, unhurried pacing and calming briefings, instead of a high-energy one.
  • You are not forced. If you want a little longer, say so.

You can choose that style in the jump designer, or read more about Rich.

What happens on the day

  1. Arrive at Dunkeswell Airfield and sign in.
  2. Complete your paperwork and one-to-one safety briefing.
  3. A short wait for your flight slot.
  4. Kit up in your jumpsuit and harness, and do a quick pre-jump video interview.
  5. Final flight line checks, then board the plane for the climb of roughly 15 to 20 minutes.
  6. Exit, freefall, and a quiet canopy ride. Your parachute is open by 5,000ft.
  7. Touch down, then a debrief and de-kit. Rich usually previews some photos with you afterwards.

Timings vary with the day and the weather. Rich confirms where to meet and when to arrive in writing when you book.

Common worries, answered

Breathing and noise. You can breathe normally in freefall. It is loud, with the wind rushing past at around 120 mph, so you and Rich use hand signals and body language, then talk again once the parachute opens.

Feeling sick. The clean air in freefall usually keeps people feeling well. If you feel queasy under the canopy, tell Rich and he will straighten up into a calm, gentle glide and take you straight down.

Backups. Every tandem system has a main parachute, an independent reserve, and an Automatic Activation Device (AAD) that can deploy the reserve automatically if parameters are met.

Nervous first-time skydive FAQs

Is it normal to be nervous before a skydive?

Yes. Being nervous is a natural reaction to doing something extraordinary. Because your jump is one-to-one rather than a high-volume session, Rich moves at your pace, talks through every step, and makes sure you feel comfortable and confident before getting on the plane.

Do I need any experience or training?

No. Before your jump Rich walks you through a one-to-one briefing covering body position, the aircraft exit and what to expect during the flight.

What if I change my mind on the day?

Tell Rich. Nothing is forced, and you can talk it through at any point before you board the aircraft.

Can I breathe and talk during freefall?

You can breathe normally through your nose or mouth. Freefall is loud, with the wind rushing past at around 120 mph, so you and Rich use clear hand signals and body language, and you can talk again once the parachute is open.

What if I feel sick or unwell?

Let Rich know or give the signal. The clean air in freefall usually keeps people feeling well, but if you feel queasy under the canopy, Rich can straighten up into a calm, gentle glide and take you straight down to land.

What backups are there if something goes wrong?

Every tandem system has a main parachute, an independent reserve parachute, and an Automatic Activation Device (AAD) that can deploy the reserve automatically if parameters are met. The equipment is maintained to British Skydiving standards.

Who can jump?

You must be at least 16 (under 18s need written consent), the weight limit is approximately 90kg (around 14 stone) fully clothed, and you complete the British Skydiving medical declaration.
